The International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA) is the universal standard for documenting human speech, capable of transcribing the sounds of any language on Earth. Yet, if you want to type it today your options are limited. You can use a website with a virtual keyboard to write out letters one click at a time, turning the process of transcription into a slow copy-paste exercise. Or, if you’re a professional linguist, your best bet might be to install Keyman: a complex tool which sits between your OS and your keyboard, designed less for IPA and more for highly complex scripts like Khmer or Tibetan.

As a programmer, the situation has always struck me as absurd. IPA is over 130 years old. It is used by linguists, singers, actors, speech pathologists, news broadcasters — basically anyone who cares about describing and reproducing human speech with scientific precision. Nevertheless, it's still harder to type than languages like Chinese and Japanese, which use complex input method editors (IMEs) to map Latin characters to thousands of logographs.

Armed with Claude, who can both vibe-code a macOS IME and draw on a deep well of knowledge about IPA, phonetics, and orthography, I set out to solve this problem. The result is IPAbet, a fast, intuitive, and native keyboard layout designed specifically for phonetic transcription.

The challenge with building IPAbet had less to do with engineering or technical issues, and more to do with design. The problem is simple: while IPA’s range of symbols is smaller than some world writing systems — officially 107 vowels and consonants, plus around 50 diacritics and other markers — this is still way more than can be represented on the standard ANSI keyboard, which has only 47 keys for letters, numbers, and punctuation.

So the question became: where do the other hundred symbols live? The answer that unlocked everything was that they should live on the *shifted* layers, and that the base layer should not change at all. An IPAbet keyboard is a completely normal US keyboard — every bare key types exactly what it always typed, so prose, code, passwords, and terminal sessions feel native. The IPA only appears when you reach for it.

The second realization was that a capital letter is a wasted key. The IPA is caseless — there are no capital phonemes — so in a transcription context, ⇧ is free real estate. IPAbet spends it on *transformation*: a capital letter typed after a glyph transforms that glyph, and the capital you choose names the articulatory move. <Combo keys="s ⇧H" out="ʃ"/> because ⇧H lenites — the same ⇧H gives you <Combo keys="t ⇧H" out="θ"/> and laxes vowels, <Combo keys="i ⇧H" out="ɪ"/>. ⇧R retroflexes: <Combo keys="t ⇧R" out="ʈ"/>. ⇧G velarizes a nasal: <Combo keys="n ⇧G" out="ŋ"/>. If you know pinyin or any romanization tradition, you already know most of these digraphs; they were chosen to match. The result is a grammar you can read rather than a chart you memorize, and it comes with a hard promise: no symbol ever costs more than two keystrokes.

Diacritics live one layer up, on Option, and they work like the dead keys you already know: <Combo keys="⌥n n" out="ñ"/>, exactly as ⌥n has worked on a Mac since before I was born. Every combining mark on the IPA chart — and the marks real orthographies need, from the Polish ogonek to the Vietnamese horn — sits on an Option key chosen for a reason it can state out loud. Somewhere in the middle of the design I noticed the Option layer had filled up with the usual squatters — © ™ ° ‰ — and none of them could say what they were *for*. That became the test every key had to pass: this is a linguist's keyboard, not a lawyer's keyboard. If a mark couldn't argue its claim in phonetic terms, it lost the key.

Under the hood, the engine is almost embarrassingly simple, and the simplicity is the point. There is no composition buffer and no mode: every keystroke looks at the text sitting before the cursor, decides on one edit, and commits it instantly — the way Korean input methods work. Type s and you have s; press ⇧H and the s you can see becomes ʃ. Because each decision is local and deterministic, the same few hundred lines of logic run identically as a native macOS input method and as a TypeScript engine in your browser — the [type-anything scratchpad](/type) and the [typing tutor](/learn) on this site are the real engine, not a demo of it. On Windows and Linux, the browser version already speaks AltGr.

Designing the layout was a months-long argument — with the IPA chart, with Unicode, and frequently with Claude — and the arguments produced laws. Shift transforms; Option appends. Opening quotes are unshifted. A repeated press cycles through a family of related marks. Some fights ended in surprising places: we shipped a "raw mode" toggle for typing code, then discovered macOS won't let you delete the plain US layout — the OS already ships a raw mode, with a better indicator than anything we could build — and deleted the feature the same evening. The Catalan ela geminada got evicted from a letter key and found its true home as a double press on the dot key: <Combo keys="l ⌥. ⌥. l" out="l·l"/>. Every one of these decisions is in the git history, argued in full.

One more thing mattered to me, because an input method is the most intimate software you can run: it sees every keystroke. So IPAbet doesn't ask for trust. The app is sandboxed with zero network entitlements — macOS itself refuses it a socket. "Fully offline" isn't a promise in the README; it's enforced by the operating system, and the code is MIT-licensed so you can check both claims.

IPAbet is out today as version 0.1.0 — a deliberately honest number. The layout may still move while early users kick the tires; when it reaches 1.0.0, the keystrokes become append-only, the way Unicode never reassigns a codepoint. Linguists keep their tools for decades, and the first number that promises your muscle memory is safe should be the one that means it.

If any of this sounds useful: [the chart](/chart) is the whole system on one printable page, [/learn](/learn) will teach the layout to your fingers in an afternoon, and [/type](/type) runs the full engine in your browser right now, no install.
