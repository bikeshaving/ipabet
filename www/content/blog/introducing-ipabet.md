The International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA) is the universal standard for documenting human speech, capable of transcribing the sounds of any language on Earth. Yet, if you want to type it today, your options are limited. You can use a website with a virtual keyboard to write out letters one click at a time, turning the process of transcription into a slow copy-paste exercise. Or, if you’re a professional linguist, your best bet might be to install Keyman: a complex tool which sits between your OS and your keyboard, designed less for IPA and more for highly complex scripts like Amharic, Khmer, or Tibetan.

This situation has always struck me as absurd. IPA is over 130 years old. It is used by linguists, singers, actors, speech pathologists, and news broadcasters — basically anyone who cares about understanding and reproducing human speech with scientific precision. Yet it’s still harder to type than languages like Chinese and Japanese, which use complex input method editors (IME) to map latin characters to thousands of logographs. The fact that a purpose-built IME did not exist for IPA struck me as both a dire need and an opportunity for innovations .

Compared to these world languages, IPA is somewhat more constrained, clocking in at officially 107 vowels and consonants, and 50 diacritics and other markers for things like syllable stress and tone.
